Budapest tips

Not everybody wants to read my whole life story, so here is what I'd tell a friend visiting Budapest: how to get around, what to eat and drink, and a bit of history and culture.

Friendly note: these are my own opinions and recommendations, not professional advice. Please check opening hours, prices and conditions before you go.

Getting around

Tip 1

Taxis, apps and public transit

Don't flag down a taxi. Order one, or take public transit.

  • Uber · Bolt
  • BudapestGO app
  • Night buses every 15 min
  • Bubi bikes · Lime · Dott scooters

Never flag down a taxi on the street, and never hop into an unknown cab: it's a guaranteed rip-off. Order your ride through an app instead. Uber and Bolt are safe, and so is any other trusted app.

Public transit is amazing, even at night: night buses run every 15 minutes in most directions.

I recommend getting a pass, either a three-day one or whatever length suits your stay. That way you don't have to buy a new ticket every time you hop from bus to tram to metro. And if you forget to validate a ticket, the inspectors will happily fine you 25,000 HUF or more (check the BKK penalty fare page for current amounts).

The easiest way to buy one is the BudapestGO app, which also has schedules, maps and a trip planner.

For short hops there is MOL Bubi, the city's bike-share run by BKK, which has just launched a new generation of bikes. Visitors can pay as they go. If you hold a BudapestGO pass valid for at least 30 days, you get a discount on the monthly Bubi pass and ten free rides a month on classic bikes.

Lime e-scooters are everywhere too. When I last checked, unlocking a scooter cost about 250 HUF, plus 50 HUF per minute, and the app has cheaper passes under "Lime-bérlet". One catch: in some central districts you can ride a scooter but can't end the ride, so check the parking map in the app first. Dott is the other scooter app you'll see around town. It shows the local price on its map, and it offers minute passes in some cities, so compare Dott and Lime before you ride. New to Dott? Enter my code 0TEW4YM in the app for a free 20-minute ride, unlock included. I get a free ride too, so everyone wins. Prices change, so look in the apps before you go.

Walking around is very safe too. Just bring a jacket when it's cold.

Tip 2

When you need some silence

Budapest is a party town. Take a break from it.

  • Buda Hills
  • Libegő chair lift
  • Children's Railway
  • Caves

Budapest is a serious party town, but sometimes a soul needs a little quiet. The Buda Hills make a perfect half-day hike, and City Park and Margaret Island are lovely for a gentle stroll.

While you're up in the Buda Hills, ride the Libegő, the chair lift that carries you up to János Hill, the highest point in Budapest. On a clear day, the view from the little tower at the top is mesmerizing. The ride itself takes about 12 to 15 minutes each way, and it's a joy.

If you're into hiking or green places, WhatsApp me and I'll share a few more tips. Here are two to start with.

Don't miss the Children's Railway, a little forest train staffed mostly by children and teenagers. Only the driver and a few others are adults. It's not far from downtown, about 45 minutes to an hour away.

There are also several caves just 30 minutes from downtown. You can visit them with a guide and no special equipment. My favorite is the Pálvölgyi stalactite cave (Pálvölgyi-cseppkőbarlang). On the way there, stop at the most amazing pastry shop, Daubner, on Szépvölgyi út near Kolosy tér.

Tip 3

The thermal bath at opening time

Set the alarm. Thank me later.

  • Opens 7 a.m. weekdays
  • City Park
  • Warm thermal water

Everybody goes to the Széchenyi thermal bath in City Park, and so should you. My tip: go right when it opens, at 7 a.m. on weekdays and 8 a.m. on weekends. Check the current hours on the bath's site. Even if getting up hurts, it's worth every yawn. At that hour it's almost all locals, and you get a glimpse of how it used to be all day, before the tourist invasion. These days it's very touristy, but first thing in the morning you still get the original experience.

It doesn't matter how cold it is outside: the water is warm, the steam rises, and the whole thing feels a little mysterious. Try it.

You can even drink the thermal water. Right by the metro station there's a drinking spot, a bit like a water bar, serving the thermal water in jugs for less than a dollar. It smells and tastes strongly of sulfur, but it's supposedly very good for the stomach.

Day trip

A Budapest tip: leave Budapest

Yes, really. Take a train out of town.

  • Train every 30 min
  • About an hour
  • Bikes to rent
  • Slovakia by bike

Here is a Budapest recommendation that tells you to leave Budapest. Every 30 minutes, a good, clean and comfortable train runs to Esztergom, a beautiful town on the Danube Bend, about an hour away.

Go for a morning that turns into an afternoon. You can rent bicycles and electric bikes very cheaply. Visit the Basilica and the old town center, then hop on a bike.

And the best part: you can bike over to Slovakia and have a beer or lunch over there, looking back at the Basilica. Two countries before lunch, and the only thing that crossed a border was you.

Eat and drink

Tip 4

Fooooood

Where the locals actually get them.

  • Lángos
  • Goulash
  • Paprikás
  • Túrós csusza
  • Salami

Of course everybody will push lángos, goulash and chicken paprikás on you.

Lángos is a round, deep-fried flatbread. Get yours at a local market. Every market has its own lángos maker, and it's usually more authentic and better than what the touristy places serve.

As for goulash, good luck finding “the best one”. It's a dish people make at home, each in their own way, and most Hungarians would struggle to name a restaurant that makes it best.

Chicken paprikás is Hungary on a plate: a little spicy, loaded with paprika and finished with sour cream. It tastes like home.

Túró, a fresh curd cheese much like cottage cheese, shows up everywhere in Hungarian cooking, from sweet to savory and everything in between. In winter, I highly recommend túrós csusza: pasta with túró, sour cream and crispy pork belly on top. It's fantastic.

Hungarian salami is also one of the best in the world, objectively. There's winter salami (téliszalámi) and paprika salami, and the best-known brands are Pick and Herz. Ask for just a few slices at the grocery store and enjoy them with a roll as a snack.

Winter salami even wears a coat of noble mold. We Hungarians seem to have a weakness for pickles, molds and noble rot.

If you like spicy sausages, Hungary is a great place to find excellent ones, usually spicier than in the neighboring countries.

Hungarian foie gras, from duck or goose liver, is also world famous. Hungary is one of the world's biggest producers, and a good share of it is processed in France. Many people are put off because the ducks and geese are force-fed to produce a large liver. Nevertheless, it's incredibly tender and delicious, served hot or cold as a pâté.

In summer and fall, Hungary has excellent fruit and vegetables, so head to a market and buy whatever is in season. Cherries, sour cherries, plums, apricots, peaches, nectarines, apples and pears are all fantastic and bursting with flavor. In winter, of course, it's mostly imported oranges and tropical fruit.

Tip 5

Wine, and Tokaji

The exported bottles are not the best of us.

  • Tokaji Aszú
  • 12–13% alcohol
  • Dessert wine

Hungary makes absolutely amazing wines, but the bottles that get exported are often not the best representation. So while you're here, try some quality reds.

Above all, try Tokaji Aszú. It's not a gimmick but a true Hungarian product of fantastic quality. It's a dessert wine that isn't too sweet, with only 12 to 13% alcohol, and it's made partly from grapes affected by noble rot, which concentrates the sugars and flavors. It has acidity as well as sweetness, so it tastes bright rather than sticky, and it's perfect with dessert.

It was one of the favorite wines of Louis XIV, the Sun King, and of many other aristocrats. He supposedly called it “the wine of kings, the king of wines”.

Tip 6

Pálinka and Unicum

Good quality: sip it. Bad quality: shoot it.

  • Pálinka up to 70%
  • Unicum
  • Sip or shoot

Pálinka is a fruit brandy, and its name is protected by the European Union as a geographical indication. It has to be made in Hungary, from Hungarian fruit and nothing else. Neighboring countries have their own versions, but Hungary probably has the widest selection of fruits: grape, plum, pear, peach, raspberry, blueberry, quince and many more.

Some pálinkas go as high as 70% alcohol, but the right one is a wonderfully delicate drink, so you know the rule: good ones you sip, bad ones you shoot.

Another drink you will meet sooner or later is Unicum. It's essentially a slightly more bitter Jägermeister. Hungarians love to drink it in combination with beer: a beer, then a shot of Unicum, then a beer, then a shot of Unicum, and you can imagine the result.

Tip 7

Drinking water

Straight from the tap.

  • Tap water is safe
  • Mineral water too

The water is absolutely safe to drink almost everywhere in Hungary, and especially in Budapest. It's actually extremely high-quality tap water.

Hungarians also love mineral water, and you'll notice it tastes much more mineral than ordinary sparkling or still water.

Fun fact

Not too cold, please

This isn't a recommendation, just an interesting fact.

A huge majority of Hungarians believe that very cold drinks will eventually give you a sore throat. As a result, you'll notice people drink their beverages warmer than in the United States, or even in Western Europe.

It goes so far that when I was little, we even had so-called winter ice cream: a cone filled with just a foam or mousse. It didn't need to be refrigerated, because it wasn't ice cream at all. Almost everybody believes it, and it passes from generation to generation.

History and culture

History

Hungarian history in two minutes

We lost, lost, lost, and we're still here.

896

Hungarians came from far to the east: nomads on horseback who lived in tents and thundered towards Europe at astonishing speed. In 896 they made it to the Carpathian Basin. You can see the leaders of the tribes on their horses at Heroes' Square.

1000

Under Saint Stephen, Hungary became a Christian country. He was ruthless with the pagans, and Hungary stayed Christian. Through generations of royal marriages, it became an integral part of Europe.

1241–1699

Then we started losing more and more wars. First came the Tatars, pretty much the Mongols, Genghis Khan and company. Then came the Turks, who were here for 150 years. You can still see some of the baths they built. After 150 years they left, and for a while we were independent, but the surrounding powers kept going back and forth, fighting one another. We lost, and lost, and lost. We have lost pretty much every battle since the 1500s.

WWI

That includes the First World War, after which Hungary lost most of its territory. Cross the border out of Hungary today and, almost anywhere you go, you're in land that used to be Hungarian.

1939–89

In the Second World War we were again on the wrong side, and lost. Then, to make things a little more exciting, we got 40 years of socialism. By the '80s it was a very light version (more on that in my story below), but in the '50s it was a hardcore dictatorship. One of our national holidays is the 1956 revolution against the Russians, when even teenagers without guns fought Russian tanks. That's seriously heroic.

1989

In 1989, with the Berlin Wall coming down, Hungary joined the democratic nations again. For some people that was a hardship, though. You have to understand that many people lost the security they had in those years, because now it's a dog-eat-dog world. That's why you see extremely rich and extremely poor Hungarians side by side.

Today

Today Hungary belongs to both NATO and the European Union, so it is firmly part of the West again, and thanks to the EU the borders have mostly disappeared. The dream of many Hungarians is to one day reach the quality of life of Western Europe.

Good to know

Topics to handle with care

Please don't call us Slavic.

There are a few topics you shouldn't bring up, or should at least approach very carefully. First: try not to call Hungarians Slavic. For some reason we take huge pride in being Finno-Ugric. Hungarian's closest relative is Finnish, though the two are still very far apart. They just share a similar rhythm.

Under no circumstances call us Slavic, or say things like "Hungary was part of Russia". It never was.

And stay away from 20th-century politics in general. You might end up in a sticky situation.

Culture

What Hungarians are proud of

Nod politely. Then have seconds.

That said, get ready. Hungarians will gladly tell you how big Hungary once was, and it's true. They'll tell you about our thousand-year history, and that's true too. Of course, most of us had little to do with it.

Hungarians are also proud of the Rubik's Cube, paprika and pálinka. Surprisingly, even sour cream and cottage cheese hold a special place in the Hungarian heart. And it's true: this kind of cottage cheese is hard to find outside this part of the world.

Speaking of food, you must try a Túró Rudi. It's a Hungarian snack that every generation adores as a child, and some of us never grow out of it, myself included. It's sweetened curd covered in chocolate: refreshing, not too sweet, and the very heart of the Hungarian soul.

At any market, stop by the pickle stalls, especially one run by the producer themselves. You'll see gigantic barrels of sauerkraut, pickled cucumbers and peppers, and even baby watermelons. They're harvested when they're only about 2 inches (5 cm) wide and completely green, and then pickled. In one sitting you can eat 5 or 6 whole watermelons.

Hungarian cuisine is rather greasy, but don't tell the Hungarians that. Just enjoy it. It's your vacation.

Fun fact

Hungarian pessimism

Prepare for the worst. Anything better feels great.

You will hear that Hungarians are deeply pessimistic. It's true. We like to prepare for the worst, so anything slightly better than the worst feels like a pleasant surprise.

Even the Hungarian anthem is quite dark: it says this nation has already paid enough for the sins of the past and the future.

Fun fact

Once we like you

Like family, and no filter.

At the same time, once a Hungarian likes you, they really like you, and may even love you like family. The emotions are far more intense, in both directions, than in the West. In that way, we're a little Mediterranean.

You also won't see as much political correctness. Most Hungarians, once they know you, tell it like it is.

Fun fact

Hungarian brains, mostly abroad

Many Nobel Prizes, few won at home.

We are very proud of our Nobel Prizes: probably among the highest counts per capita in the world. Almost none were won in Hungary, though. The experiments needed money the country didn't have, and these geniuses found a warmer welcome abroad.

Vitamin C, first isolated from Hungarian paprika, is one of the Nobel-winning discoveries.

The ballpoint pen was invented by a Hungarian.

So was the Rubik's Cube.

And at least partially, the hydrogen bomb and the computer were invented by Hungarians as well.

Fun fact

The Hungarian language

Hard to learn, easy to read out loud.

The Hungarian alphabet is essentially the Latin alphabet, with a lot of accents added. We have four different Os (o, ó, ö, ő) and four different Us (u, ú, ü, ű). A few sounds are written with letter pairs, like ty, which outsiders find nearly impossible to pronounce.

Hungarian is extremely difficult. Even after you learn the rules, you're not finished, because almost every rule comes with exceptions.

The one piece of good news: Hungarian is phonetic. Once you master how each sound is spelled, the written word never surprises you, unlike English, where the same spelling can sound completely different.

Who am I?

Me and the world

First of all, I don't take myself seriously.

I try to be as nice to people as I can. I believe in karma, and there's nothing mystical about it: be nice to people and it tends to come back to you. Don't, and eventually someone won't put up with it.

I work in hospitality, yet I'm something of a loner. I spend a lot of time on my own and often travel solo. I used to travel with my mom a lot, too. Now I have Tobi, my dog. I started fostering him in the Cayman Islands in 2024 and officially adopted him in May, right before moving back to Hungary. I couldn't leave him behind, so he came with me to Budapest.

Chapter 1

Born in socialist Hungary

Bananas showed up three or four times a year.

I was born in socialist Hungary. Don't be alarmed. In my lifetime it was more of a joke than a hardcore dictatorship: minor inconveniences, a perfectly fine childhood, and everybody more or less equal. But I sure missed bananas, which showed up maybe three or four times a year. Honestly, some things were better back then. I'm not asking for the '80s back, though. Just my youth.

My father was a sportsman who competed abroad and brought home the Western goodies our stores lacked, so I was a little privileged: pineapples, Kinder eggs and treasures like a Mars bar. I grew up on the Buda side of Budapest.

Chapter 2

Sunday lunches at grandma's

Sour cherry pies, cottage cheese palacsinta, and loud debates.

My childhood memories come in two kinds. On one side, my parents' fights, which thankfully ended once they realized they just didn't fit.

On the loving side, my grandpa and especially my grandma, my angel until she passed away during COVID. I'll never forget the Sunday lunches and the debates around the table: grandpa, me, my brother and sometimes my father, while grandma made sour cherry pies and palacsinta, Hungarian crepes filled with cottage cheese. No Michelin-star chef can beat those flavors. They're engraved in my taste buds and my heart forever. Grandma was one of my biggest fans.

Chapter 3

Dad, the divorce and the Pest side

I cried for days. Then the neighborhood became the coolest in town.

My father is very competitive, so his expectations weren't easy to live up to. He was a serious race walker, ranked 16th in the world in Montreal, and he still holds a couple of Hungarian records from the '80s.

People from Buda are proud of living on the greener, fancier side of the city, and I was no different. When my parents divorced, my mom and I moved to the Pest side, and I cried for days. Then, right when I hit my teens, that same area became the coolest part of town.

My father later remarried a woman a good 30 years younger. To anyone who says an age gap ruins a relationship, I beg to differ: they're still together, with three kids in their twenties. He has now spent far more time with her than with my mom. Good for them.

Chapter 4

The troublemaker

Mustard sandwiches, the Hungarian Parliament on TV, and a brother with straight A's.

I was, of course, the troublemaker. My brother, two and a half years older, was the ideal kid: straight A's, five languages, every parent's dream. And then there was me, the lazy ass eating mustard sandwiches after school and watching the Hungarian Parliament on TV instead of doing my homework.

I've always been curious about the world, but school was tough unless I had an amazing teacher. When something grabs me, though, I give it everything.

I was never a big football hooligan, but a friend and I went to quite a few games. Once, about 30 years ago, we went to watch Kispest play Ferencváros (FTC) at Ferencváros's big stadium. There were maybe 100 or 200 of us Kispest fans against thousands upon thousands of Ferencváros fans.

And I had a great idea: a road flare, the kind you light at the roadside after an accident so people can see you in the dark. The police even promote them, so I figured, why not? It turns out there are plenty of reasons why not. At a football match, flares are very illegal. Before I could even light it, the police came and arrested both of us. They threw us in a car, took us to the nearby police headquarters and put us in actual cells.

I thought that was the end of it. Thankfully I could call my brother to pick me up later, and I didn't have to tell my parents. But six months later my mom almost fell off her chair: she got a letter saying she had to come to the police station with me, because I was being questioned as a suspect.

In the end I never got into real trouble: no charge, no sentence. It was very scary, though, and rather funny: I thought I was the big guy walking in there to light this thing up, and I ended up crying in a cell, hiding it from my mom, until it all came out six months later in a letter from the police. That was 30 years ago, and I can laugh about it now.

Chapter 5

Learning hospitality by doing

No degree, 150 gadgets and a plush hamburger.

My very first job, apart from working for my father, was on the opening team of TGI Fridays Budapest. I did my best to outdo the waiters on TV: about 150 gadgets on me, from a camo hat with little Lego men crawling all over it to a plush hamburger and fries on my apron, plus pins, badges and everything in between.

I had no formal training in hospitality. I just loved hanging out with foreigners, and I made plenty of friends in the expat community and among foreign students.

Chapter 6

Canada, round one: Saskatoon

From 2 million people to 200,000, minus 45 C, and my first New York.

I met a foreign lady who quickly became my wife, and I moved to Saskatoon, Saskatchewan. From a city of 2 million to 200,000 in the middle of the prairies, and I loved it beyond words: minus 45 C, bison, and the Canadian life I'd only seen in movies.

I got married at 23, and it lasted exactly 7 years, at least on paper. Still, there are good memories, and the wedding is the one that always comes up.

We were as poor as church mice, as we say in Hungarian. Instead of a fancy hall, we rented the community hall at Saskatoon's Forestry Farm Park and Zoo for $200, with bison and moose as the backdrop. Instead of catering, everyone brought their best dish, and we ended up with about 15 amazing dishes. The total came to about $1,000, and it was more memorable than any $100,000 wedding.

I went to university without really knowing what I wanted, so I picked political science, which interested me. Everyone said you need a degree to get ahead.

On the side, I worked at some really cool places. At the University of Saskatchewan faculty club I had free rein over the salads, inventing crazy dressings and cheese plates with no worries about food cost, because the point was keeping the professors happy. At a tiny Irish-style corner bar I did everything from morning omelettes to bartending at night. Unforgettable. As a new immigrant, I made real friends among the regulars who came in for a pint of Guinness.

Canada also made a big dream come true: the Big Apple, for real. I first went to New York in 2003, not long after moving to Saskatoon, and I've been back about 60 times since. New York has been a big part of my life, with happiness, sadness and everything in between.

New York gave me something else too: my hero. K. is a tall, elderly New York diner server, the kind of person who makes the job look like an art. I'm the one hugging him.

Chapter 7

Ottawa

Apples, rotten tomatoes and a diploma.

Life eventually took us to Ottawa, Ontario, for my wife's studies, so I switched universities too and continued at Carleton University. A proper job was nearly impossible, since I spoke English and Hungarian but no French. So I became a produce clerk at a grocery store while studying. Interesting times, to put it politically correctly: living at the poverty line in a foreign country, barely making ends meet, and getting yelled at by my boss for not turning the apples the right way.

Show the photo

It didn't help that my wife later left for her doctorate studies on Long Island, New York, so I was alone in Ottawa with my university and my rotten tomatoes. My whole last year there I was on my own, and every three weeks I went to see her in New York. That's one reason I've been to New York so many times.

Ottawa had its fun sides too. The neighborhood we lived in was quite rough, which made it a great place for police car spotting: from our balcony we could see the numbers of the police cars. And in winter it was fun to skate to school on the Rideau Canal.

I did finish Carleton, in political science. I never worked a single day in politics, but it sharpened my critical thinking. So Ottawa wasn't only the dark side of being poor, desperate and depressed: I became a Canadian citizen there and earned my university degree. Those were real achievements.

Chapter 8

Back in Budapest, and then Nobu

A turn upwards, out of nowhere.

On a vacation back home in Budapest, I simply couldn't make myself return to Ottawa. So at 30, I stayed and moved in with my mom. At least I wasn't alone, and I wasn't destitute.

Then, out of nowhere, things turned around. I asked friends where to look for hospitality work, and they said Nobu was coming. I had no clue what Nobu was, but I got hired at the last second, and when they mentioned four weeks of training, I knew it was serious. For the opening, the whole area around the Hotel Kempinski was blocked off, red carpet everywhere, and literally every Hungarian celebrity I knew of was there.

And that's not all: Robert De Niro, a part owner of Nobu, came too, and by coincidence so did Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie with their kids. I couldn't believe how much my life had changed in just a couple of months.

Chapter 9

Round two: Canada, alone

Can I do the Canadian life alone?

After another year at Nobu, I had an idea: go back, all the way to Saskatoon, this time alone. Almost a dare to myself: can I do the Canadian life alone?

The hardest part was Mazsi, my little wiener dog. His name means "raisin" in Hungarian. Not long after I got back to Saskatoon, he had an accident, and I never got to say goodbye to him. It's still sad even to think about it.

I landed back in Saskatoon and found work at a Brazilian steakhouse, and I had some really fun times there. I barely had to carry plates. It's a little crazy that my wife was also in Saskatoon by then, and we never once ran into each other. Maybe because she was vegetarian and I worked in a Brazilian steakhouse.

I had a few shorter relationships in those days, and of course they didn't end well. Then one day the mother of our hostess came in for dinner. I literally asked the hostess if she could hook me up with her mom, and to my great surprise, she did.

Her mom, J., and I became a couple, and we had some really great times in Saskatoon. We even got two dogs, a brother and sister named Daisy and Duke.

Chapter 10

The Cayman Islands

The beach was 30 seconds away.

We had a wonderful year and a half in Cayman. We couldn't believe we lived right on the beach after Canada: an apartment with a swimming pool, a swim-up bar, and turquoise Caribbean water 30 seconds away. Snorkeling after work was an absolute dream.

We even had a two-bedroom apartment, so we rented out one of the rooms, the one with its own bathroom, on Airbnb. It covered our rent, and we met a lot of wonderful people. I guess I was practicing hospitality on the side, too.

Later, as they say, the Cayman curse caught up with us: most couples who go there together break up. It happened to us as well, but we stayed very good friends.

Then I met T., a lady from the Philippines who worked with me at the Ritz. We started dating and moved in together. Quite a change.

Life in the Cayman Islands was quite an experience. You feel you don't have to prove yourself to anyone. All year round it was just a T-shirt and shorts, and it was real freedom. It also gets boring, and many people there become professional drinkers.

It took me six years, but I finally went scuba diving too, and it opened up yet another world. After six years you also get island fever, which is why we kept escaping. Miami and New York were the typical getaways, and I also went to Cuba a couple of times, with my mom and with a couple of coworkers.

Cuba: rum, cigars and a trip back in time.

Cuba was something else, and absolutely amazing. It's a different world, really like going back in time, and I loved it so much that I went back about three times. I'm very happy my mom joined me on one of those trips, and we made some great memories.

In Havana we took a rickshaw ride, and the driver was delighted that we agreed. But the two of us together were a lot for one poor rickshaw driver to pedal, and the chain broke. They had to call another guy to fix it. We were unbelievably embarrassed.

And I always noticed a surprising number of single guys who were very interested in the Cuban culture and the architecture of Havana... wink, wink.

Not everything went smoothly, though. On my very first night, a girl offered me a beer, and I got drugged. The last thing I remember is two girls at our apartment trying to come in, and the landlady shouting "no, no, no chicas!" Next thing I knew, I woke up and saw that I had thrown up all over the wall. I have never been so embarrassed. They explained that they had put some medicine in my drink, and they were actually very nice about it: for $10 the whole problem was fixed. They cleaned the wall and even ironed my shirt. In the end nothing happened, and it became one of my favorite funny stories.

Chapter 11

COVID in Hungary

The last plane from London.

Then came COVID. I had to go home to Hungary, and T. went back to the Philippines. We didn't break up, and we made it through the two years apart.

Getting home was scary. It was pretty much the last plane from London, a British Airways repatriation flight, and as a European I could get on it. It was really weird: all the flight attendants wore hazmat suits and simply tossed sandwiches in Ziploc bags at us. In London there was almost no one at the airport, and the workers seemed to be saying that this was the end, the last plane out. In Hungary, at the airport, they gave me a gigantic red sticker to put on my door: suspected COVID patient, do not enter.

By then my grandma was in an old folks' home, so her apartment was empty and my mom gave it to me to use. It's the very apartment where I am right now. I stuck the red sticker on the door and stayed inside for two weeks. Friends told me the neighbors downstairs were already speculating about who the COVID patient was, and I was worried they'd lynch me.

I did my best to educate my grandma, but there was a COVID outbreak in her old folks' home anyway. About two months later she passed away. They said it wasn't only COVID; dementia played its part too. I'm just really happy I got to see her a few times before she died, because if I had stayed in Cayman, I never would have.

I probably wasn't used to Hungary anymore. I listened to the ambulances, which were loud and constant, and checked the numbers every day: how many died, how many were hospitalized. Hungary was doing really badly. I didn't work. I went for walks, saw my mom, and tried to get her as many good masks as possible.

For the vaccine I could only get the Russian Sputnik, and I really wanted Pfizer. So I had an idea: hop on a train, go to Romania and pick it up. It totally worked. I convinced my mom and her boyfriend, B., to come too, and we all got our Pfizer shots. I was so scared that I probably have more vaccinations than anybody: I count at least nine.

In 2022, when the borders opened, T. and I both went back to the Cayman Islands.

Two years later, in 2024, I started going to the humane society in Cayman to take a dog out for walks. One day the dog I usually walked wasn't there, and they recommended Tobi, who they thought would be a good fit. I took him for a walk, and I think I fell in love with him. I asked if I could keep him for a weekend, and that weekend turned out to be the last time he stayed at the shelter. From then on I fostered him, and I only made it official in May, right before moving back to Hungary.
