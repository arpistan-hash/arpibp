Who am I?

Me and the world

First of all, I don't take myself seriously.

I try to be as nice to people as I can. I believe in karma, and there's nothing mystical about it: be nice to people and it tends to come back to you. Don't, and eventually someone won't put up with it.

I work in hospitality, yet I'm kind of a loner. I spend a lot of time on my own and travel a lot by myself. I used to travel a lot with my mom too. Now I have Tobi, my dog. I adopted him in the Cayman Islands about three years ago, and when I left I couldn't leave him behind, so he came with me to Budapest.

Chapter 1

Born in socialist Hungary

Bananas showed up three or four times a year.

I was born in socialist Hungary. Don't be alarmed. In my lifetime it was more of a joke than a hardcore dictatorship: minor inconveniences, a perfectly fine childhood, and everybody more or less equal. But I sure missed bananas, which showed up maybe three or four times a year. Honestly, some things were better back then. I'm not asking for the '80s back, though. Just my youth.

My father was a sportsman who competed abroad and brought home the Western goodies our stores lacked, so I was a little privileged: pineapples, Kinder eggs and treasures like a Mars bar. I grew up on the Buda side of Budapest.

Chapter 2

Sunday lunches at grandma's

Sour cherry pies, cottage cheese palacsinta, and loud debates.

My childhood memories come in two kinds. On one side, my parents' fights, which thankfully didn't last long, until they realized they just didn't fit.

On the loving side, my grandpa and especially my grandma, my angel until she passed away during COVID. I'll never forget the Sunday lunches and the debates around the table: grandpa, me, my brother and sometimes my father, while grandma made sour cherry pies and palacsinta, Hungarian crepes filled with cottage cheese. No Michelin-star chef can beat those flavors. They're engraved in my taste buds and my heart forever. Grandma was one of my biggest fans.

Chapter 3

Dad, the divorce and the Pest side

I cried for days. Then the neighborhood became the coolest in town.

My father is very competitive, so his expectations weren't easy to live up to. He was a serious speedwalker, ranked 16th in the world in Montreal, and he still holds a couple of Hungarian records from the '80s.

Buda people are proud of living on the greener, fancier side of the city, and I was no different. When my parents divorced, my mom and I moved to the Pest side, and I cried for days. Then, right when I hit my teens, that same area became the coolest part of town.

My father later remarried a woman a good 30 years younger. Anyone who says an age gap ruins a relationship, I'd beg to differ: they're still together, with three kids in their twenties. He's now spent far more time with her than with my mom. Good for them.

Chapter 4

The troublemaker

Mustard sandwiches, the Hungarian Parliament on TV, and a brother with straight A's.

I was, of course, the troublemaker. My brother, two and a half years older, was the ideal kid: straight A's, five languages, every parent's dream. And then there was me, the lazy ass eating mustard sandwiches after school and watching the Hungarian Parliament on TV instead of doing my homework.

I've always been curious about the world, but school was tough unless I had an amazing teacher. When something grabs me, though, I give it everything.

I was a mess, but a good mess. Never aggressive, never in real trouble, other than unannounced trips and coming home a bit worse for wear from various venues. The late '90s were the peak of Budapest's underground party scene, and I'm forever grateful cell phones arrived just after my party years.

Chapter 5

Learning hospitality by doing

No degree, 150 gadgets and a plush hamburger.

My very first job, apart from working for my father, was on the opening team of TGI Fridays Budapest. I went out of my way to out-flair the waiters on TV: about 150 gadgets on me, from a camo hat with little Lego men crawling all over it to a plush hamburger and fries on my apron, plus pins, badges and everything in between.

I had no formal training in hospitality. I just loved hanging out with foreigners, and I made plenty of friends in the expat community and among foreign students.

Chapter 6

Canada, round one: Saskatoon

From 2 million people to 200,000, minus 45 C, and my first New York.

I also met a foreign lady who quickly became my wife, and I moved to Saskatoon, Saskatchewan. From a city of 2 million to 200,000 in the middle of the prairies, and I loved it beyond words: minus 45 C, bison, and the Canadian life I'd only seen in movies.

I got married at 23 and lasted exactly 7 years, at least on paper. Still, there are good memories, and the wedding is the one that always comes up.

We were as poor as church mice, as we say in Hungarian. Instead of a fancy hall, we rented the community hall at Saskatoon's Forestry Farm Park and Zoo for $200, with bison and moose as the backdrop. Instead of catering, everyone brought the dish they make best, and we ended up with about 15 amazing dishes. The total came to about $1,000, and it was more memorable than any $100,000 wedding.

I went to university without really knowing what I wanted, so I picked political science, which interested me. Everyone said you need a degree to get ahead.

On the side I worked in some really cool places. At the University of Saskatchewan faculty club I had free rein over the salads, inventing crazy dressings and cheese plates with no worries about food cost, because the point was keeping the professors happy. At a tiny Irish-style corner bar I did everything from morning omelettes to bartending at night. Unforgettable. As a new immigrant, I made real friends among the regulars who came in for a pint of Guinness.

Canada also made a big dream come true: the Big Apple, for real. I first went to New York in 2003, not long after moving to Saskatoon, and I've been back probably 80 times since. New York has been a big part of my life, with happiness, sadness and everything in between.

Chapter 7

Ottawa

Apples, rotten tomatoes and a diploma.

Life eventually took us to Ottawa, Ontario, for my studies at Carleton University. A proper job was nearly impossible, since my second language was Hungarian, not French. So I became a produce clerk at a grocery store while studying. Interesting times, to put it politically correctly: living at the poverty line in a foreign country, barely making ends meet, and getting yelled at by my boss for not turning the apples the right way.

It didn't help that my wife was studying in New York by then, so I was alone in Ottawa with my university and my rotten tomatoes.

I did finish Carleton, in political science. I never worked a single day in politics, but it sharpened my critical thinking.

Chapter 8

Back in Budapest, and then Nobu

A turn upwards, out of nowhere.

On a vacation back home in Budapest, I simply couldn't make myself return to Ottawa. So at 30, I stayed and lived with my mom. At least I wasn't alone, and I wasn't destitute.

Then, out of nowhere, a huge turn upwards. I asked friends where to look for hospitality work, and they said Nobu was coming. I had no clue what Nobu was, but I got hired at the last second, and when they mentioned four weeks of training, I knew it was serious. For the opening, the whole area around the Hotel Kempinski was blocked off, red carpet everywhere, and literally every Hungarian celebrity I knew of was there.

And that's not all: Robert De Niro, a part owner of Nobu, came too, and by coincidence so did Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie with their kids. I couldn't believe how much my life had changed in just a couple of months.

Chapter 9

Round two: Canada, alone

Can I do the Canadian life alone?

After another year at Nobu, I had an idea: go back, all the way to Saskatoon, this time alone. Almost a dare to myself: can I do the Canadian life alone?
